# Bio Buddy knowledge file
Anything above the first ## line is ignored. These are general intro-bio study notes.

## Mitosis vs meiosis: what is the difference?
Mitosis makes two genetically identical daughter cells from one parent cell. It has one round of division and the daughter cells are diploid (2n), the same as the parent. Cells use it for growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction. Meiosis makes four genetically different cells from one parent cell. It has two rounds of division (meiosis I and meiosis II) and the daughter cells are haploid (n). Cells use it to make gametes (sperm and eggs). Only meiosis has synapsis (homologous chromosomes pairing up) and crossing over. Quick memory trick: Mitosis = Make identical copies. Meiosis = Make sex cells with a Mix of genes.

## Stages of mitosis (PMAT)
Before mitosis, the cell copies its DNA during S phase of interphase. Then remember PMAT. Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible, and the spindle starts to form. Metaphase: chromosomes line up along the middle of the cell, called the metaphase plate. Anaphase: sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite ends of the cell. Telophase: nuclear envelopes re-form around the two sets of chromosomes and the chromosomes relax. Cytokinesis, the splitting of the cytoplasm, usually overlaps with telophase and makes two separate cells.

## Stages of meiosis I and meiosis II
DNA is copied once, before meiosis I. Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes. In prophase I, homologs pair up and cross over. In metaphase I, homologous pairs line up together at the middle. In anaphase I, the homologs are pulled apart, but sister chromatids stay together. Telophase I and cytokinesis give two haploid cells. Meiosis II separates sister chromatids and works like mitosis. There is no DNA copying between meiosis I and II. The final result is four haploid cells, each with single-chromatid chromosomes.

## Homologous chromosomes
Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes, one from each parent, that carry the same genes in the same order. They are the same size and shape, but they can carry different versions of a gene, called alleles. Humans have 23 pairs of homologous chromosomes, for 46 in total. Homologs are not identical copies of each other. Sister chromatids are different: they are exact copies made by DNA replication and are joined at the centromere. In meiosis I, homologs pair up and then separate. In meiosis II and mitosis, sister chromatids separate.

## Sex chromosomes vs autosomes
Humans have 22 pairs of autosomes (non-sex chromosomes) and 1 pair of sex chromosomes. Females typically have two X chromosomes (XX) and males typically have an X and a Y (XY). The X and Y are different sizes and carry different genes, but they pair up at small matching regions during meiosis so they can separate properly. Every egg carries an X chromosome. A sperm carries either an X or a Y, so the sperm determines the sex of the offspring. Genes on the X chromosome are called X-linked genes.

## How does meiosis create genetic variation?
Meiosis produces variety in three ways. First, crossing over happens in prophase I, when homologous chromosomes swap pieces of DNA and make new combinations of alleles. Second, independent assortment happens in metaphase I, when each homologous pair lines up and separates independently of the other pairs. With 23 pairs, humans can make over 8 million different chromosome combinations in a gamete. Third, random fertilization means any sperm can combine with any egg. Together these make siblings different from each other and from their parents.

## Haploid vs diploid (n vs 2n)
Haploid (n) means a cell has one set of chromosomes. Diploid (2n) means a cell has two sets, one from each parent. In humans, body cells (somatic cells) are diploid with 46 chromosomes, and gametes are haploid with 23 chromosomes. Meiosis cuts the chromosome number in half, going from diploid to haploid. Fertilization joins two haploid gametes to make a diploid zygote, which restores the full number.

## Steps of cellular respiration
Cellular respiration turns glucose and oxygen into ATP, carbon dioxide, and water. It has four stages. 1) Glycolysis, in the cytoplasm: glucose is split into 2 pyruvate, with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH. 2) Pyruvate oxidation, in the mitochondrial matrix: pyruvate becomes acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2 and making NADH. 3) Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle), in the matrix: acetyl-CoA is broken down, making CO2, a little ATP, NADH, and FADH2. 4) Electron transport chain and chemiosmosis, on the inner mitochondrial membrane: NADH and FADH2 drop off electrons, oxygen accepts them to make water, and most of the ATP is made. The total is roughly 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, depending on the textbook.

## How to remember the steps of cellular respiration
Use the sentence "Go Play Kickball Everyday" for Glycolysis, Pyruvate oxidation, Krebs cycle, Electron transport chain. Then attach one location and one fact to each step. Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm and needs no oxygen. Pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle happen in the matrix. The electron transport chain happens on the inner membrane, and oxygen is the final electron acceptor. Try drawing a mitochondrion from memory and labeling where each stage happens. Then say out loud what goes in and what comes out of each stage.

## Steps of DNA replication
Replication starts at an origin of replication. 1) Helicase unwinds and unzips the double helix, forming a replication fork. 2) Single-strand binding proteins keep the strands apart, and topoisomerase relieves twisting ahead of the fork. 3) Primase makes a short RNA primer. 4) DNA polymerase adds new nucleotides to the primer, always building in the 5' to 3' direction. The leading strand is made continuously. The lagging strand is made in short pieces called Okazaki fragments. 5) Another DNA polymerase replaces the RNA primers with DNA. 6) DNA ligase seals the gaps between fragments. Enzyme names here are from bacteria, and eukaryotes use different polymerases for the same jobs.

## Why is DNA replication called semiconservative?
Semiconservative means each new DNA molecule is made of one old (original) strand and one newly made strand. The original double helix unwinds, and each old strand acts as a template for a new partner strand. This was shown by the Meselson-Stahl experiment, which used nitrogen isotopes (heavy 15N and light 14N) to tell old DNA from new DNA. Two other models were ruled out: conservative replication (one all-old molecule and one all-new molecule) and dispersive replication (mixed pieces of old and new in both strands).

## How to read a metabolic pathway diagram
Start by finding the first molecule (the substrate) and the final molecule (the product). Each arrow is one reaction, and the enzyme is usually written next to the arrow. Read the arrows in order and ask what goes in and what comes out at each step. If an enzyme is missing or blocked, the molecule just before the block builds up and the molecules after it drop. Watch for feedback inhibition, where the final product shuts down an enzyme early in the pathway so the cell doesn't overproduce it. Dotted lines or T-shaped arrows often show inhibition. Always check whether the question asks what happens upstream or downstream of the change.

## Best ways to study and remember biology
Active recall works better than rereading: close your notes and write or say everything you remember, then check what you missed. Spaced repetition means reviewing in short sessions over several days instead of cramming. Teach the concept out loud as if explaining it to a friend. Draw processes like mitosis, meiosis, and respiration from memory instead of copying diagrams. Make mnemonics and analogies for ordered lists like the stages of mitosis. Practice with questions, and write down why each wrong answer is wrong.
